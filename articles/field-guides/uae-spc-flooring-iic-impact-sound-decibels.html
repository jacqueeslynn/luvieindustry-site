UAE SPC flooring: IIC 55 is not a 55 dB reduction

A UAE apartment flooring tender may contain three impressive-looking acoustic numbers: 18 dB, IIC 55 and 19 dB. Ranking them from largest to smallest would produce a meaningless shortlist. Before choosing SPC, flexible vinyl or another finish, identify whether each number describes an improvement, a floor–ceiling assembly rating or sound heard inside the room. This guide uses published third-party data to explain that distinction, not to certify a Luvie model.

Quick answer

IIC 55 is an impact insulation class, not a promise to reduce sound by 55 dB. Keep IIC, impact-sound reduction and in-room walking noise in separate columns. Compare products only after the acoustic consultant confirms that the test assembly and metric answer the project requirement.

Why 55 cannot be subtracted from 18

ASTM E989 describes a method for rating impact noise transmitted through floor–ceiling assemblies. For results from ASTM E492 testing, the single-number rating is called IIC. It is therefore an assembly rating rather than an amount to deduct from a room’s measured sound level. See the ASTM E989 scope.

The 18 dB and IIC 55 entries on the Polyflor page are not competing claims. They describe different acoustic metrics. Writing “55 dB sound reduction” in a comparison spreadsheet would change the meaning of the manufacturer’s information. Likewise, adding a quoted underlay improvement to a floor’s IIC is not a valid way to calculate a new assembly rating.

What a one-decibel headline difference does not establish

The table contains 18 dB and 19 dB figures, but it does not establish that one installed UAE apartment will be quieter than another. First compare the original test reports, including the reference floor, ceiling arrangement, backing and assessment method. A technical sheet may summarize the result without describing enough of the assembly for a project-specific decision.

Do not turn the numerical difference into a percentage improvement in perceived comfort. A buyer also needs to distinguish sound transmitted downstairs from the sound of footsteps heard by the person walking on the floor. The latter is a different question, and a favourable result for one metric does not automatically settle the other.

Build an acoustic bid schedule that survives substitution

Ask the project acoustician or responsible consultant to identify the required metric and acceptance route before suppliers submit alternatives. Put the exact flooring SKU, attached backing, permitted separate underlay, slab and ceiling information alongside each result. Include report number, date and the model covered. Mark missing assembly details as unresolved instead of replacing them with an internet estimate.

For refurbishment, give the supplier the existing construction information that the project team can verify. If the old ceiling will be removed, a report relying on a particular ceiling cannot simply be carried into the new specification. Ask which parts of the original test arrangement are relevant and which differences need specialist assessment.

Once an option is approved, record its component names in the purchase order. A substitute pad with the same thickness is not automatically acoustically or mechanically equivalent. Obtain flooring-manufacturer approval for the proposed assembly, especially where a click joint is supported by a resilient layer.

Separate a demonstration from acceptance

A showroom walkover can reveal a sound that the buyer dislikes, but it is not an IIC test. A phone recording is affected by microphone processing and room conditions. Use demonstrations to discuss expectations, then use the specified evidence and any required site testing for acceptance. This avoids asking an informal sample trial to prove something it was never designed to measure.
