SPC Flooring Samples in Iraq: A Retailer Loan System

A flooring retailer in Iraq may send the same display board to an architect, a contractor and a homeowner before anyone orders. The sales problem is not simply having enough samples. It is knowing which product each visitor actually approved, whether the sample remains representative, and which questions are still open. A controlled sample-loan system connects a physical board to a usable quotation without turning a decorative example into an unsupported performance promise.

Short answer

Give each loan sample an identity, record its condition and return date, and separate appearance approval from technical suitability. A sample loan should create a documented next step, not merely an unanswered request for a price.

Label the sample without inventing a specification

Record the supplier model, finish code, sample date and whether the board represents the current offer. Keep dimensions, backing and installation method in an attached specification sheet rather than handwritten guesses. If the retailer has only an appearance sample, say so. A short plank cannot establish the behaviour of a complete floor. Do not infer fire classification, wear resistance, emissions or suitability for a commercial space from its weight or the click sound. Ask the supplier to confirm which documents belong to the exact product before presenting technical claims to a customer.

Make the loan useful for the Iraqi project

Ask where the sample will be viewed: a Baghdad apartment, a shop being refurbished or a contractor office. These are project contexts, not proof of one national installation condition. Request photographs of adjacent finishes and the intended lighting. Ask the borrower to record the approved finish code rather than send only a phone photograph. Phone screens and automatic white balance change appearance. A customer who likes the colour still needs the installer to review the substrate, transitions and applicable product instructions. Put those unresolved items beside the appearance decision so the sales team knows what to follow up.

Inspect the board before it circulates again

After return, check the surface and the edges and record any damage. A visibly worn sample can misrepresent a new finish; a damaged locking edge can also confuse an installation demonstration. Keep demonstration pieces separate from appearance-approval pieces when appropriate. Use suitable storage so samples do not rub against grit or other sharp objects. General manufacturer care guidance, such as the Tarkett reference, illustrates why handling matters, but its cleaning prescriptions must not be copied onto a different brand. Obtain the actual supplier care sheet and retire samples whose identity or condition is uncertain.

Turn approval into a specific quotation request

The next message should identify the selected finish, approximate area, room use and destination, plus whether trims and installation are needed. Quote unresolved items as unresolved rather than promise that a matching product is immediately available. When a sample predates current stock, request confirmation before collecting a purchase commitment. Keep a copy of the approval and the supplier response together. This gives two sales colleagues a shared history and prevents the customer having to repeat the entire discussion. A simple register is sufficient if each entry is complete and somebody owns the next action.

Before you request a quotation

  • Assign a unique sample ID and current product reference.
  • Record the borrower, project, return date and next action.
  • Separate appearance approval from installation and compliance review.
  • Inspect returned boards and retire unidentified samples.

Frequently asked questions

Does approving a sample approve the whole floor system?

No. Appearance selection does not resolve substrate conditions, installation details, accessories or required project evidence.

Should every loan receive the same follow-up?

No. An architect selecting a finish needs a different next step from a contractor requesting a project quantity and accessory quotation.
